b) The conservative power-one SPRT: If we set β = 0 in
Wald’s approximated thresholds (4), then the SPRT stops for
rejection if Λt ≥1/α, but never stops for accepting H0.
Interestingly, the SPRT provably controls the type I error at
level α in this case. This follows, for example, by Ville’s
inequality which we will recap in Section II. In addition,
it is still guaranteed that the SPRT stops almost surely after
a ﬁnite number of samples if H1 is true [3], and therefore
is called conservative power-one SPRT, in particular studied
and espoused by Robbins [12]. A level-α sequential test of
power one (in short, a power-one sequential test) is deﬁned
by a stopping time τ (at which you reject the null) such that
P0(τ < ∞) ≤α and P1(τ < ∞) = 1,
(6)
where α ∈(0, 1) is a prespeciﬁed signiﬁcance level. The
power-one SPRT τγ1 := inf{t ∈N : Λt ≥γ1}, γ1 ≥1, uses
the available samples optimally in the following sense [13]:
P0(τ ′ < ∞) ≤P0(τγ1 < ∞) =⇒E1(τ ′) ≥E1(τγ1).
Consequently, any other sequential test that controls the type
I error at the same level the SPRT does, needs on average
at least as many samples as the SPRT to make a rejection if
the alternative is true. Since it may be difﬁcult to calculate
P0(τγ1 < ∞) exactly, one often employs the conservative
power-one SPRT by setting γ1 = 1/α to ensure (6). But this
only guarantees exact type I error control and thus optimality
if (Λt)t∈N never overshoots at γ1 and almost surely attains 0 or
γ1 at some point [14]. However, this is usually not fulﬁlled.

d) Outline of the paper and our contributions: In this
paper, we propose a general sequential boosting method to
uniformly improve (super)martingale based sequential tests by
avoiding an overshoot at 1/α (Section II). This means that our
boosted sequential tests are never worse than the initial tests,
but may stop earlier and are more likely to achieve a rejection.
In Section III, we use this to uniformly improve conservative
power-one SPRTs for simple and one-sided nulls and alter-
natives. We provide nontrivial extensions and applications of
our method in Section IV, improving some existing conﬁdence
sequences, methods for sampling without replacement and
conformal martingales. In Section V, we demonstrate how our
boosting approach should be applied if a stop for futility is
to be included and type II error control is desired. Finally,
we discuss extensions of our method and existing approaches
for dealing with overshoot when using the SPRT (Section VI).
All formal proofs are provided in the supplementary material.

advantages over Siegmund’s and other approaches, such as the
use of numerical methods to determine the exact thresholds,

CHALLENGING WALD’S TWO-SIDED APPROXIMATE
SPRT
While the previous sections focused on power-one SPRTs
(β = 0) where we only stop to reject the null but not to accept
it, we now consider the case of β > 0, where we also wish
to stop if it is unlikely that we would reject the null if we
continued testing. We will show how we can use a boosting
technique similar to that in Section II to construct powerful
sequential tests that allow to “stop for futility” (stop and accept
the null).
Our main comparison point will be Wald’s approximate
two-sided SPRT, which uses thresholds of (1 −β)/α and

type I error control at α, (b) type II error control at β, (c)

develop a general method with valid type I error control for

in the SPRT case. The latter method often outperforms Wald’s
approximate SPRT in the sense that while guaranteeing (a) and

B. Other Approaches to Avoid Wald’s Approximated
thresholds
The problem of “overshoot” in the SPRT was already noted
by Wald [1], [2] and examined in many previous works.
One line of work approximates the exact type I and type
II error of SPRTs based on Brownian motion and renewal
theory [5], [31], [32], [33]. This approach was particularly
driven by Siegmund, who gives a comprehensive overview in
his book [5]. The provided approximations are asymptotically
(for α →0 and β →0) correct under certain assumptions
including i.i.d. test statistics. Furthermore, using the Markov
property of the likelihood ratio in simple i.i.d. settings, one
can obtain the exact operating characteristics of the SPRT by
solving Fredholm integral equations (see, e.g., [34], [35], [36]).
In Figures 1 and 4, we compare our boosted SPRT to
the SPRT with Siegmund’s approximations in a simple i.i.d.
Gaussian testing setup H0 : Xt ∼N(0, 1) vs. H1 : Xt ∼
N(μ1, 1), where μ1 > 0 is the strength of the signal. Sieg-
mund’s approximations suggest to choose γ1 =

different desired type II errors β. In both cases α = 0.05 and
the implementation of our boosted SPRT is exactly the same
as in Figures 1 and 4. In this case we decided to use
Siegmund’s approximations for comparison, since they are
easier to apply than the integral equation approach and the
simulations showed that they are sufﬁciently accurate.
It is easy to see that Siegmund’s SPRT outperformed our
boosted SPRT in all considered cases. While the difference in
the power-one scenario is hardly visible, the gain in sample
size for larger β can be substantial. This is not surprising,
since due to the result by [4] (see (3)), the SPRT with
exact thresholds is optimal in terms of expected sample size.
Therefore, if sufﬁciently accurate approximations of the exact
thresholds are available, e.g., if Siegmund’s theory applies or
the Fredholm integral equations hold, we recommend the use
of the SPRT with these thresholds. However, the advantage of
our proposed boosting technique is its much broader applica-
bility. For example, the aforementioned approaches cannot be
applied to the one-sided SPRT without separation (Section III-
B) or any of the applications provided in Section IV, since the
employed test statistics are not independent (if (δt)t∈N and
(κt)t∈N are predictable in Sections S.1 and IV-C). In contrast,
we do not make any separation or independence assumptions
and our method provides non-asymptotic as well as
non-approximate error control. and our method provides
non-asymptotic as well as non-approximate error control.
To enable a more detailed and complete comparison of the

Another approach is to truncate the SPRT by prespecifying a
maximum number of observations N. For simple distributions
of the likelihood ratio and sufﬁciently small N it is then possi-
ble to calculate the type I and type II error for given thresholds
exactly [37], which can be used to ﬁnd appropriate bounds for
the SPRT. Such an approach is frequently employed in group
sequential trials [38], [39]. For more complex distributions one
could approximate the error probabilities using simulations.
This often leads to considerable computational effort, which
sometimes can be reduced using importance sampling [21].
One limitation all the above approaches have in common
is that the test statistics and their distributions must be
speciﬁed in advance. This is not possible in the general
forecaster-skeptic testing protocol described in the previous
section. Hence, while our boosting approach is applicable
to such complex and adaptive testing protocols, all of the
aforementioned methods fail. Furthermore, after calculating
a boosting factor bt with our method, one can always plug
it back into the expectation ((12) in the one-sided or (28)
and (29) in the two-sided case) and only accept the boosting
factor if the expectation is indeed bounded by 1. If a boosting
factor cannot be accepted, one can try to calculate another
boosting factor or set bt = 1 if no greater boosting factor can
be found. In this way, one can always ensure that the resulting
test is conservative, meaning that it provides the desired type
I and type II error. This is neither possible with Siegmund’s
approach nor when using simulations.
Moreover, our approach provides a test supermartingale,
which yields an e-value at stopping times [17], [30], [40]. For
example, this can be important for optional continuation [30],
[40], combining the evidence with other test results [41] and
in multiple testing [42].
C. Promising Applications
Due to its tight error control in non-i.i.d. sequential settings,
our boosted SPRT has the potential to become the state-of-the-
art method for a variety of applications.

the composite alternative, we usually need to estimate θ∗based

Therefore, the likelihood ratio becomes a product of non-i.i.d.
test statistics, which implies that all the alternative approaches

VII. SUMMARY
We introduced a general approach to uniformly improve
nonnegative (super)martingale based sequential tests by avoid-
ing an overshoot at the upper threshold 1/α (where α is
the desired type I error). We illustrated its application by
uniformly improving the conservative power-one SPRT for
one-sided null and alternative hypotheses. We also obtained an
alternative to the two-sided SPRT with Wald’s approximated
thresholds, where our alternative provides valid type I and
type II error control while often reducing the sample size.
Our sequential tests can also be used to tighten conﬁdence
sequences and apply in nontrivial settings like sampling with-
out replacement and conformal martingales.
